Most tank water heaters in Antioch, CA last somewhere between eight and twelve years, and that number drops with hard water, high pressure, or a tank nobody ever flushed. Most tanks give a few warnings near the end: a slow leak from the bottom of the tank, rusty hot water, rumbling that grows louder every month, or a supply that runs out sooner each winter. A leak from the tank body is the clearest signal of all. When the glass lining gives out and the steel corrodes through, there is no part to replace, and a slow weep can turn into a flooded floor with no notice.

Choosing the replacement is where most of the value is. The old heater's size is only a starting point, since families grow, bathrooms get added, and a 40-gallon tank that was fine a decade ago may now run out every morning. Tanks get sized by first-hour rating, tankless units by gallons per minute at winter temperature rise, and we explain what each option costs up front and month to month. A like-for-like gas or electric tank is usually the simplest and least expensive swap. Tankless and heat pump models cost more up front but can lower operating costs, and they come with their own requirements for gas line size, venting, electrical capacity, or room to breathe. You get the facts, then you decide.

The swap itself follows a checklist, not a guess: old tank drained and removed, fresh connectors and a working shutoff put in, gas lines leak-tested and fitted with a sediment trap, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. If local code requires a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, they go in, and the permit is pulled whenever one is needed. We finish by filling and purging the tank, checking draft and carbon monoxide on gas models, setting the thermostat near 120 degrees, and showing you around the new heater. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • Sediment noise that keeps getting louder
  • Hot water running out faster every month
  • Water seeping out from under the tank jacket
  • Visible corrosion around the pipe connections or burner area
  • Repeated service calls on the same aging heater
  • Rusty or brown water from the hot side only
  • Operating costs climbing on an older, less efficient heater
  • A water heater that is more than about ten years old

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Keep the space around a heat pump model clear so it can draw heat from the room
  • Keep household water pressure in a safe range to protect the tank and its fittings
  • Write the install date on the tank so you know when to plan the next replacement
  • Flush the new tank once or twice a year to keep sediment from building up
  • Descale a tankless unit every year, or more often with hard water
  • Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safety and slower mineral buildup
  • Inspect the anode rod every few years; replacing it can add years to the tank
