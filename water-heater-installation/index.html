Many of the installation calls we get in Antioch, CA are not replacements at all. A family adds a bathroom and needs more capacity. A homeowner wants the heater out of a hall closet and into the garage or basement. A remodel calls for a second unit at the far end of the house, or an all-electric conversion means trading gas for a heat pump model. What these jobs share is new work: gas lines that must be sized for the load, vents that must be routed and pitched correctly, circuits that must match the heater, and water lines that must be run cleanly. That is the planning Antioch Water Heater does before any installation begins.

Sizing and placement shape most of the outcome. For a tank, the first-hour rating is the number we compare against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Location comes next. A gas heater needs a vent route and combustion air, a heat pump heater needs open space and a condensate drain, and a whole-house electric tankless unit usually needs a big dedicated circuit and matching panel capacity. Any heater placed above a finished ceiling needs a drain pan, and a gas unit in a garage may need to sit on a raised platform where code requires it. You get the trade-offs in plain terms, with no pressure.

The install itself follows a checklist: gas lines sized, run, and leak-tested with a sediment trap at the heater, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, circuits and breakers matched to the heater's load, and water lines connected with shutoffs and dielectric protection where dissimilar metals meet. The relief valve gets a full-size discharge line to a safe point, and we add an expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps wherever code calls for them, including the permit when one is required. To finish, we fill and purge the tank, test draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, set the thermostat near 120 degrees, and walk you through the new setup. Why This Job Calls for a Pro

A new water heater installation is really several trades in one job. The gas piping must be sized for the heater plus every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested until it is leak-free. Venting has to follow the manufacturer's rules for diameter, length, pitch, and clearance to combustibles, or carbon monoxide can spill back into the house. Electric and heat pump units need a circuit, breaker, and wire gauge matched to their load, and whole-house electric tankless units can draw more current than many panels have to spare. Then come the water-side details: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that vary by location.

A professional installation covers the whole job, not just setting the heater. Sizing by first-hour rating or flow rate, tested gas piping, a vent routed to spec, a circuit matched to the load, and the expansion tank, pan, or straps your code requires are all included. Before we go, gas units are checked for draft and carbon monoxide. You end up with an installation that is safe, properly sized, and ready for inspection. If a smaller or simpler heater fits your needs better, you will hear it from us directly.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
  • Leave working space around the heater, and never stack storage against it
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
  • Nothing flammable belongs near a gas burner or its air supply
  • Schedule regular maintenance, including an anode rod check
  • Glance at the pan and discharge pipe every month or two; water there means it is time to call
